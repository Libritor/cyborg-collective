How a scan is made

From a headset's pictures to a Gaussian splat, a solid and a print file: the chain, what has been checked and what has not, and every tool as a plain file.

A thing or a place is photographed with a Meta Quest 3S headset. A laptop turns the photographs into a Gaussian splat and, for an object, into a closed solid sliced for a 3-D printer, and the result goes onto this site. Everything it is made with is on this page.

One command on the laptop the headset is plugged into. Without -Live or -Publish everything stays on that laptop; nothing is ever sent to a printer.

The chain

Six steps. The two with a dashed edge happen only when asked for, because they are public.

  1. The headset keeps picturesIts app keeps a picture from the passthrough camera every 5 cm or 6 degrees of head movement, with the headset's own position and direction for it and an 80 × 60 grid of depth rays behind it. Pulling the trigger on a thing names it as the object. The headset has no lidar: its depth comes from its two tracking cameras.
  2. Live, if asked forWhile it scans, the app also sends one depth reading per ray to the laptop over the USB cable. The laptop keeps one per 2 cm of surface and passes them through the relay; the live page draws each as a Gaussian the moment it arrives. These are placed, not trained.
  3. The laptop trains the splatThe headset's positions are used as they are, with no structure from motion. The pictures are cropped round the object and Brush trains the Gaussians for 8,000 steps, about two minutes on a laptop's graphics card. A tenth of the pictures are kept back and compared with what the splat draws from their positions.
  4. From splat to solidThe splat's depth is drawn from every camera position and voted into a grid: outside where enough cameras see past a cell, surface where they see it, solid where none could see, so the body is closed. It is cut flat on the surface it stood on.
  5. SlicedThe solid is scaled and sliced for a Bambu H2C with the slicer's command line, which reports the time and the grams. Nothing is sent to a printer.
  6. Published, if asked forOpen3D reads the trained splat and writes it compressed, about a tenth of the size; that file, the solid, a render and the print files go into this site's repository. Open3D does not make the splat: it has no training. The camera pictures are not published.

The tools

Plain files, as they are used; nothing here is built. Python (numpy, scipy, numba, Open3D, scikit-image, Pillow, playwright), PowerShell, and one C# script for Unity 6 with Meta's XR packages. Their defaults name folders in the user's home folder. The viewer is view.html and the live page live.html: their source is the page itself, with scans.css.

How these pages are honest
  • Seen by a headset, or a test: every scan carries one of the two labels. A test was drawn on a computer from a model whose shape is known, so that the result could be measured; it is not passed off as a headset scan.
  • What is published is what was computed: the splat, the solid, renders of them and the print files. The camera pictures stay with the person who took them.
  • A place photographed from one spot is shown as that: a view to look round from where it was taken, with gaps away from that spot and no solid. A room model beside it is an estimate, and its card says where its shape comes from.
  • Live Gaussians are placed, not trained: one for each reading of the headset's depth sensing, drawn as it arrives. The trained splat of the same sitting comes minutes later. The relay passes the live ones on and keeps none.
  • The headset has no lidar: its depth is what it works out from its two tracking cameras.
  • Not yet done, and said where it matters: a walk all the way round one object with the headset; the live view from the headset itself (made-up rooms have been sent so far); AR and VR inside the headset from this site; a print.
